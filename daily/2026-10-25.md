3. **[Introducing Kimi K3.1 Pro](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Oct/23/kimi-k31-pro/)**

   Introducing Kimi K3.1 Pro Kimi K3.1 Pro landed on the Kimi API today - it's not yet open weights, but Moonshot promise "Full model weights will be released by Nov 4, 2026". They're also offering a "Highspeed" version that's 2-3x faster, at twice the price (except for input token cache hits, which are priced the same). The US dollar pricing is $2.00/million tokens for input and $10/million for output - the same as GPT-6.1 Sol and half the price of Claude Opus 5.5. Moonshot's own benchmarks show Kimi as approximately level with Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra - beating them on a few benchmarks, coming close on others. Artificial Analysis scored Kimi at 55 - ahead of Claude Fable 5.1, GPT-6 Astra, and Gemini 4 Argon (all at 53) and behind only Claude Opus 5.5 (58). This is the first time a (soon-to-be) open weight model has scored this highly against the frontier. I tried it out via the Moonshot API using the llm openai endpoint command I added to LLM 0.32 back in August: llm openai endpoint \ https://api.moonshot.ai/v1 \ --key kimi \ -m kimi-k3.1-pro \ "Generate an SVG of a pelican riding a bicycle" \ --responses \ -o reasoning_effort low \ &gt; kimi-k3.1-pro-pelican-low.stdout.txt \ 2&gt; kimi-k3.1-pro-pelican-low.stderr.txt llm openai endpoint can run prompts against any OpenAI-compatible endpoint but doesn't log the results, hence the extra options to save stdout and stderr (the reasoning trace). Here are the pelicans I got for Kimi K3.1 Pro on the three reasoning levels it supports - low, medium, and max. This is max: [图片: https://static.simonwillison.net/static/2026-10-23/image-2.png] For comparison, I ran fresh pelicans against Kimi K3 on those three reasoning levels. 1. **[Rethinking Sequence Compression with Test-time Regression](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31574)**

   Sequence compression is a fundamental operation and a critical information bottleneck in efficient language and multimodal models, and yet prevailing approaches based on pooling or learned projections remain underperforming. We revisit sequence compression through the lens of test-time regression: input tokens define a regression problem whose fitted coefficients form the compressed representation. Importantly, the regression problem is learned end-to-end via backpropagation, while its coefficients are computed efficiently in closed form during the forward pass. This leads to TTR-Compressor, a general alternative to conventional pooling and projection-based compression. We evaluate TTR-Compressor as the compressor for block-wise sparse attention and the visual token merger for multimodal large language models. On block-wise sparse attention, TTR-Compressor improves RULER performance over attention-based compressors by a large margin. For visual token merging, it consistently outperforms the standard MLP merger and matches its performance with 55.6% fewer visual tokens. These results establish test-time regression as an effective primitive for sequence compression in efficient language and multimodal models.

2. **[Making MLLM Latents Matter: Factor-Aware Predictive Learning for Cinematic Video Generation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30926)**

   Cinematic video generation requires both temporal inference from limited inputs (i.e., sparse rather than frame-by-frame guidance) and factor-aware control of character, scene, action, and camera. Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) offer useful priors for both capabilities, but their representations are not automatically actionable for video generation: a Video Diffusion Transformer (DiT) may bypass them through native conditioning pathways, and using them does not ensure either factor-aware control or generation-compatible temporal prediction. We propose Factor-Aware Predictive Learning (FAPL), which establishes effective MLLM latent use as a shared prerequisite and then targets the two capabilities with complementary learning signals. First, latent-only generator grounding trains the DiT with a frozen MLLM and connector, strengthening the DiT's use of MLLM latents for generation. Second, for factor-aware control, paired videos specify a target-factor change and non-target preservation, and factor-span replacement trains the DiT to realize this relationship in its output. Third, for temporal inference, text-only and text-plus-complete-video observations share a video target, and the trained DiT is then frozen while the MLLM and connector are optimized through it. This final stage trains limited-input representations for generation while retaining complete-observation reconstruction as a complementary training mode. Extensive experiments demonstrate our consistently better performance on three cinematic video generation tasks (i.e., sparse-to-video, factor-aware editing, and multi-shot generation) over baselines and validate FAPL's contributions to MLLM feature use, temporal control, and factor coordination.

   来源：HuggingFace Papers

3. **[Inherited, Not Invented: Tracing the Provenance of Wait in Reasoning Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30882)**

   Reinforcement learning (RL) is credited with teaching reasoning models to write {wait} before reconsidering a step, yet large RL accuracy gains do not require the word. This view can confuse what RL discovers with what a model inherits from its training data. We trace the word through open models' training histories, reading it with label-free indices of frequency, position, form and usage and an injected-error test, all computed from sampled outputs. We find that zero-RL almost never coins the marker: on bases that lack the word, RL raises MATH-500 accuracy substantially while {wait} stays near absent. It arrives with training data: mid-training in OLMo-3, and fine-tuning on reasoning traces installs it in most models whose base lacks it. Where the base already uses it, both released RL-Zero recipes (OLMo-3, MiMo) multiply it, whereas KL-free GRPO from the same base does not; OLMo-3's recipe wears down its form and makes it a more sensitive but more liberal alarm; RL after supervised fine-tuning barely changes it, and our GRPO recipe erases a marker seeded by fine-tuning. Finally, we show that marker frequency alone does not indicate RL-induced reasoning and caution against LLM-as-a-judge revision metrics, as automated judges can reverse human-labelled trends.
