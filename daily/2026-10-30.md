2. **[Building a multiplayer game world with autonomous Fable-class models](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Oct/29/how-i-built-the-most-ai-pilled-company-in-the-world/)**

   How I Built the Most AI-Pilled Company in the World Don't be put off by the clickbait headline, this piece by Jacob Sansbury describing how they are building Spawn is a fascinating exploration of the bleeding edge of what's possible with agentic engineering against frontier models. Spawn is a massively multiplayer gaming environment where players can use prompts (to the in-game agent, Savi) to modify the game world. I fired it up on my phone for a few minutes and was able to prompt a castle into existence, then a dragon, then ride the dragon to fly around that castle. [图片: https://static.simonwillison.net/static/2026-10-29/IMG_9406.webp] By Jacob's telling, Spawn is now being developed autonomously by a swarm of Fable-powered agents costing over $3 million in tokens per month ("we spend 30x payroll on AI tokens"). Our agents change the live code directly and ship to production hourly, so they read every error, bug report and metric themselves. About 60% of our work starts from a production error, a Savi bug report (Savi is the AI you build games with in Spawn), something odd in our logs, or a feature request. Savi files bugs herself. When a user hits one, or Savi hits one in the product, she files it. It goes straight to an agent that decides whether it's a real bug to fix, something we won't fix, or intentional design that needs a decision. Usually it becomes a change, delivered to that user within an hour, with a message in the same chat. This is continuous deployment turned up to 11: A loop this tight only works if it never stops, so we treat an hour without a ship as a SEV-0, our most severe kind of incident. Unshipped changes pile up, and every problem already in production lasts longer. The feedback slows down too. An agent can only keep a few hours of work in its head, so shipping faster than that actually helps it stay coherent. [...] We can roll back a broken change in 30 seconds, exactly where it's broken and nowhere else. When a monitor or a log alert fires, it starts or wakes an agent that owns the incident, and we break things on purpose to test that those recovery paths work. My eyebrows raised at least a dozen times reading this. I have trouble imagining this style of development working on software with higher stakes for when something goes wrong, but for a game this appears to be working surprisingly well. Jacob says that Fable 5 (and then 5.1) was the model that made this level of autonomy possible. Opus 5.5 doesn't quite hack it for them, and when they tried GPT-6 Astra to apply fixes from a security review everything stopped working: Every feature had been disabled and marked to be turned on again once the security fixes were done. Astra had gone through our whole product and made the insecure things secure by turning them off. These kinds of extreme experiments are why I'm excited about game development as a substrate for exploring the edges of what today's coding agents can do. 4. **[Quoting Ted Sanders](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Oct/28/ted-sanders/)**

   In general, LLM performance is incredibly multidimensional. There's no guarantee of a fixed ordering where the model that is best at math is also the model that's best at jokes. Despite that complexity, much of the variation in performance is explained by just a single variable: model size. In general, models that are bigger will (a) be more performant / reliable, (b) cost more per token, and (c) have better recall of esoteric facts. As you note, our models are ordered: Luna &lt; Terra &lt; Sol &lt; Astra [ ... ] These days, version number signifies a few things at once: the order of release, the relationship to other releases, and the magnitude of improvement. So something like 6.1 would indicate it was launched after 5.6, may share some similarities to other 6.1 models in terms of training data / personality (which is why it's not 5.7), and isn't a giant enough leap to merit the headlines and expectations that would come with a 7. &mdash; Ted Sanders , explaining OpenAI's model naming conventions Tags: generative-ai , gpt , openai , ai , llms , ted-sanders

6. **[The October inflection point for vibe game development](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Oct/28/vibe-game-development/)**

   I've written before about how November 2025 was an inflection point for coding agents - the point where Opus 4.5 and GPT-5.1 crossed a tipping point that meant their respective coding agents became reliable daily drivers for software engineers. I think we've reached a new inflection point in October 2026, and this time it's for vibe coding computer games . Tree Appreciator Emberwick Battle Drones Pumpkin Farmer, aka Quarter Sun Cove Four times as insulting What happens if someone actually puts the work in? You should build a game! Claude Opus 5.5 in particular exhibits meaningful improvements in several areas needed to build a compelling game: 3D modeling Music generation (see Scrimshaw Jukebox ) Game design (!) That last one is particularly important. Models have been able to create something that looks like a game for quite a while now, as seen in my previous experiments . The challenge has been getting to a game that's actually fun to play . Game design is a deep subject area. I won't pretend to be well versed in it - I know enough to have respect for it as a craft. Creating a novel, compelling gameplay loop which challenges players at the right level and provides a satisfying learning curve is a fine art. I didn't think the models could do it. Then I tried telling Claude Opus 5.5 to "Make it a LOT more fun"... and it did . In the past few weeks I've vibe coded (in the classic sense of the term - no looking at the code at all) four games that I've genuinely enjoyed playing. I attended Foo Camp in Berkeley over the weekend and met several people who were having similar experiences. Building games that play well is shockingly easy now. The new challenge is coming up with concepts for games that are novel and delightful and that wouldn't exist without AI assistance. I haven't done an exhaustive study, but my current impression is that GPT-6 Astra isn't quite there yet. It can absolutely build a game, but its sense of fun doesn't match that of Opus 5.5. I expect OpenAI will get there by the end of the year. Here are the four game projects that have convinced me that the models are now capable of producing fun games with very little guidance.
