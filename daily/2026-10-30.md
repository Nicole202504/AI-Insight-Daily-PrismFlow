2. **[Quoting Ted Sanders](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Oct/28/ted-sanders/)**

   In general, LLM performance is incredibly multidimensional. There's no guarantee of a fixed ordering where the model that is best at math is also the model that's best at jokes. Despite that complexity, much of the variation in performance is explained by just a single variable: model size. In general, models that are bigger will (a) be more performant / reliable, (b) cost more per token, and (c) have better recall of esoteric facts. As you note, our models are ordered: Luna &lt; Terra &lt; Sol &lt; Astra [ ... ] These days, version number signifies a few things at once: the order of release, the relationship to other releases, and the magnitude of improvement. So something like 6.1 would indicate it was launched after 5.6, may share some similarities to other 6.1 models in terms of training data / personality (which is why it's not 5.7), and isn't a giant enough leap to merit the headlines and expectations that would come with a 7. &mdash; Ted Sanders , explaining OpenAI's model naming conventions Tags: generative-ai , gpt , openai , ai , llms , ted-sanders

4. **[The October inflection point for vibe game development](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Oct/28/vibe-game-development/)**

   I've written before about how November 2025 was an inflection point for coding agents - the point where Opus 4.5 and GPT-5.1 crossed a tipping point that meant their respective coding agents became reliable daily drivers for software engineers. I think we've reached a new inflection point in October 2026, and this time it's for vibe coding computer games . Tree Appreciator Emberwick Battle Drones Pumpkin Farmer, aka Quarter Sun Cove Four times as insulting What happens if someone actually puts the work in? You should build a game! Claude Opus 5.5 in particular exhibits meaningful improvements in several areas needed to build a compelling game: 3D modeling Music generation (see Scrimshaw Jukebox ) Game design (!) That last one is particularly important. Models have been able to create something that looks like a game for quite a while now, as seen in my previous experiments . The challenge has been getting to a game that's actually fun to play . Game design is a deep subject area. I won't pretend to be well versed in it - I know enough to have respect for it as a craft. Creating a novel, compelling gameplay loop which challenges players at the right level and provides a satisfying learning curve is a fine art. I didn't think the models could do it. Then I tried telling Claude Opus 5.5 to "Make it a LOT more fun"... and it did . In the past few weeks I've vibe coded (in the classic sense of the term - no looking at the code at all) four games that I've genuinely enjoyed playing. I attended Foo Camp in Berkeley over the weekend and met several people who were having similar experiences. Building games that play well is shockingly easy now. The new challenge is coming up with concepts for games that are novel and delightful and that wouldn't exist without AI assistance. I haven't done an exhaustive study, but my current impression is that GPT-6 Astra isn't quite there yet. It can absolutely build a game, but its sense of fun doesn't match that of Opus 5.5. I expect OpenAI will get there by the end of the year. Here are the four game projects that have convinced me that the models are now capable of producing fun games with very little guidance. All four are built in HTML and JavaScript and run entirely in the browser. Tree Appreciator The smallest game I found to be genuinely delightful is Tree Appreciator . I used the regular Claude chat interface for this - it didn't even need Claude Code. I built this on a whim while we were driving along Skyline Boulevard looking at trees. My prompt was: Build a three js game called "Tree appreciator" - it shows you two algorithmically generated trees and the game mechanics is about appreciating the differences between them. Think hard about how to make sure it is both fun and beautiful. I've found that telling models to use three.js works really well. Claude doesn't have its own image generation model but is capable of building full-color 3D models that look and feel excellent. This game required no iteration or follow-up prompting at all - I got the game in a single shot. It gave me back a charming little mobile-friendly spot-the-difference game. You can play that here . Battle Drones This is by far my most successful project, which I wrote about last week . It's a Command &amp; Conquer-style Real-Time Strategy game where two robot armies (or four as of Saturday ) build units, harvest scrap, and battle over territory. This is also the project that convinced me that Opus 5.5 knows what "fun" is. The first versions were built back in July using Claude Fable 5. It got me to a workable 2D Command &amp; Conquer-style game, but it wasn't great to play. I prompted Opus 5.5 to "Make it a LOT more fun" and then later to "Port this game to three.js" and the game was transformed. There's a blow-by-blow account (prompts included) in my write-up . 1. **[TAO: Tool-Aware Joint Scheduling for LLM-Based Agentic Serving](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37230)**

   Agentic services alternate between model inference on clustered GPUs and external tools on distributed CPUs. In this scenario, existing systems (1) overlook tool-return readiness in service evaluation, wasting limited resources, and (2) use heuristic strategies to decide admission and placement on GPUs, resulting in suboptimal scheduling decisions. To bridge this gap, we present TAO, a tool-aware joint scheduling framework for LLM-based agentic serving. Its value interface combines near-term tool readiness, node-specific cache credit with time-aware decay, and token footprint; a dynamic program jointly selects and places waiting sessions under capacity constraints. Across the 12 reported model-workload-concurrency settings, TAO reduces latency by up to 26.8%, reduces mean time to first token (TTFT) by up to 36.3%, and increases decode throughput by up to 29.5% relative to the best evaluated state-of-the-art baseline for each metric.
